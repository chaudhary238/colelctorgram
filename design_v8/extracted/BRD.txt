1. Document Control & Purpose
This Business Requirements Document (BRD) defines the product vision, scope, functional requirements, and phased delivery plan for CollectorHub — a community-oriented social platform for hobby collectors. It is intended to align the product, engineering, content, and marketing workstreams on what we are building, for whom, in what order, and how success will be measured.
The document is deliberately phased. Phase 1 targets an initial community of 10,000 engaged users across four collectible categories; Phase 2 expands categories, monetisation, and platform depth once retention and liquidity are proven.

2. Executive Summary
Collecting is a large, fast-growing, and deeply social hobby — yet collectors today are scattered across general-purpose tools that were never built for them: Instagram for showcasing, WhatsApp and Facebook Groups for community and buy/sell/trade, eBay and marketplace apps for transactions, and Discord/Reddit for discussion. No single product combines identity, community, discovery, and trusted peer-to-peer trading in one place tuned to a collector's specific interests.
CollectorHub closes that gap. A collector creates a profile, selects their interests, and builds a collection portfolio — adding items quickly from a built-in catalogue (search or barcode scan) and confirming what they actually own with an in-app photo. Any owned item can be listed for sale or trade in a tap; pre-orders carry a value and timeline. A personalised master feed surfaces new releases, community discussions, marketplace listings, and events relevant to them. They join communities, message other collectors directly to transact, confirm completed deals, and build reputation through transaction-linked trust signals — not just a popularity count — so trading feels safe.
Phase 1 launches with four categories — Action Figures, Designer Toys & Blind Boxes, Model Kits & Lego, and Diecast — and a target of 10,000 engaged users. Early content and activity are seeded by admin and managed dummy accounts (new releases, news, discussions), with a deliberate, measured shift to user-generated content as the community reaches critical mass. The product is built around a clear daily return loop: new release posts, marketplace listings, event announcements, community discussions, and collection showcases — all amplified by system-driven notifications.
The core bet
Collectors will return daily if the platform reliably delivers two things general tools cannot: (1) a feed tuned precisely to what they collect, and (2) a trusted place to trade with people whose reputation they can verify. Everything in Phase 1 serves those two outcomes.

3.4 Design Principles
Interest-first. Interest-first, not follower-first. The experience is organised around what you collect, so a brand-new user with zero followers still gets a rich, relevant feed on day one.
Trust is a feature. Trust is a feature. Reputation, vouches, and visible seller history are first-class, because peer trading lives or dies on trust.
Seed, then step back. Seed, then step back. Admin and dummy accounts prime the pump; the product is engineered to hand the microphone to real users as fast as possible.
Realistic to build. Realistic to build. Phase 1 is intentionally scoped to a shippable MVP; ambitious capabilities (live streams, grading, escrow, in-app payments) are deferred to Phase 2.

4. Market Context & Opportunity
The collectibles category has moved from niche hobby to mainstream cultural and economic phenomenon. The designer-toy / blind-box secondary market alone was valued at over US$1.8 billion in 2025, propelled by phenomena such as Pop Mart's Labubu. In India — a priority market given the community focus (e.g. communities like 'Indian Toy Maniacs') — the toys market was valued at roughly US$2.77 billion in 2025 and the broader collectibles market is forecast to grow at ~10% CAGR through 2033.
Across these markets, collector behaviour is consistent: people want to show off what they own, learn about new and upcoming releases, find others who share their niche, and buy/sell/trade — increasingly peer-to-peer. Today they stitch this together across Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook Groups, Discord, Reddit, eBay, and category marketplaces. That fragmentation is the opportunity.
4.1 Why Now
Mainstreaming of collecting — blind boxes, Lego adult sets (e.g. Lego x Pokémon from 2026), diecast, and designer toys are pulling in new adult collectors.
Secondary-market momentum — strong resale demand makes a trusted peer marketplace genuinely valuable.
Community fragmentation — collectors are underserved by general social tools; no dominant collector-native social network exists in the target geographies.
Trust deficit — peer trading on Facebook/WhatsApp is rife with scam risk; a reputation/vouch layer is a clear differentiator.

6.2 The Open Question: Trading Cards & Sneakers — Phase 1 or Phase 2?
You asked whether to delay Trading Cards and Sneakers to Phase 2. Below is a neutral trade-off analysis for the decision; a recommendation is intentionally not imposed.
Option A — Delay Cards & Sneakers to Phase 2
Pros
 | Cons / risks
 | 
Tighter focus → better feed relevance and community density with a smaller audience.
 | Misses two of the largest, most liquid collector markets early.
 | 
Lower build & moderation scope for MVP; faster to ship.
 | Cards & sneakers have the most direct, well-funded competitors (Mantel, StockX, GOAT) — ceding ground.
 | 
Avoids categories that demand heavy trust infrastructure (grading, authentication, escrow) on day one.
 | Some users collect across categories; absence may feel incomplete.
 | 
Cleaner signal on which of the four toy-led categories truly drive retention.
 | Re-engaging card/sneaker communities later costs marketing effort twice.
 | 

Option B — Include Cards & Sneakers in Phase 1
Pros
 | Cons / risks
 | 
Captures the biggest collector audiences from launch; faster path to 10,000 users.
 | These categories expect authentication/grading & condition standards that are hard to do well at MVP.
 | 
More marketplace liquidity and trade volume early.
 | Higher scam/counterfeit exposure (esp. sneakers) → trust risk before reputation system matures.
 | 
Broad appeal for cross-category collectors.
 | Dilutes focus; harder to seed quality content across six categories with limited admin/dummy capacity.
 | 

Suggested decision framing (for you to confirm)
A defensible middle path: launch Phase 1 with the four toy-led categories to perfect the feed, community, and vouch mechanics on a manageable scope, while running a lightweight 'interest waitlist' toggle for Cards and Sneakers to measure latent demand. Promote whichever shows the strongest pull into early Phase 2 — by which point the trust infrastructure (reputation, condition standards) they require will be more mature. The BRD is structured so either decision can be slotted in without rework.

8. Functional Requirements
This section specifies requirements feature by feature. Each requirement carries a priority: Must (M) — required for Phase 1 launch; Should (S) — strongly desired in Phase 1; Could (C) — Phase 1 if capacity allows; Won't-now (W) — explicitly Phase 2.
8.1 Core Data Model & Object Relationships
Before the feature requirements, this subsection defines the four core objects the product is built on. Keeping these separate (rather than treating a 'post' as the unit everything derives from) is the structural foundation for the sell flow, marketplace, trust, and metrics. See §18 for the reasoning.
Object
 | What it is
 | Key relationships & lifecycle
 | 
Item
 | A single collectible in a user's collection — the source of truth for what a user owns or wants. Created from the catalogue (search/scan) or free-text, with an ownership-verification status.
 | Belongs to one user. Status: owned / wishlist / pre-order. An owned Item can be offered as a Listing. Persists after sale as 'previously owned' (trade history).
 | 
Listing
 | An Item offered for sale or trade. Holds price, condition, quantity, trade-willingness, status.
 | Derived from one owned Item. Status: available / reserved / sold / closed. Closing/selling updates the parent Item. Surfaced in the Marketplace and (optionally) announced via a Post.
 | 
Post
 | Social content (Showcase / Discussion / Review). Drives feed discovery and conversation.
 | Authored by a user; may reference an Item or Listing; may be posted into a Community. Deleting a Post does not delete the underlying Item/Listing.
 | 
Deal
 | A record that a transaction occurred between two users (Phase 1 completes off-platform).
 | Links a buyer, a seller, and a Listing/Item. Created when a party marks 'sold to @user' and the other confirms. Closes the Listing, writes trade history, and unlocks mutual rating/vouch.
 | 

In short: the Item is the atomic unit. Listings and Posts both reference Items; Deals record what actually changed hands. This decoupling means selling is an action on an item (not a content-creation act), trust is built from completed Deals, and a wishlist Item can be matched against incoming Listings.

13. Trust & Safety
Because CollectorHub facilitates peer-to-peer trading, trust and safety is a product pillar, not an afterthought. Phase 1 establishes the human-moderated foundation; Phase 2 adds automated and financial protections.
13.1 Phase 1 Foundations
Transaction-linked trust. Trust is built from completed Deals — trade vouches require a confirmed transaction; raw social endorsements are weighted lower and capped. Profiles surface transaction-linked signals (deals, rating, response time, account age).
Ownership verification. Tiered ownership verification (Claimed / Shown / Verified) with in-app-only live capture; listing requires Verified. Possession is not genuineness (§8.3 CO-11).
Photo integrity. Perceptual-hash duplicate-image detection (anti photo-theft) and watermarking of verified photos.
Reporting & blocking. Report content & users from items, listings, posts, profiles, and DMs; block users from conversations; peer 'this is a stock photo' reporting.
Moderation. Admin moderation queue to review reports, remove content, and suspend/ban bad actors.
Transaction clarity. Clear guidance that Phase 1 transactions complete off-platform, with safety tips for meet-ups and payments.
13.2 Phase 2 Enhancements
Reputation scoring algorithm over the Deal/rating graph; verified/tiered badges.
Genuineness verification (item tells, NFC 'tap to verify', expert/grading partnerships) — distinct from possession.
Dispute resolution flow and (with in-app payments) escrow protection.
Fraud/scam detection and seller KYC for higher-value categories.

18. Reasoning for Changes (v1.1)
This section documents why v1.1 departs from the originally specified flows. The brief was reviewed not just for completeness but for whether each flow was the optimal way to achieve the goal. The changes below cluster around two findings: (1) the original model conflated content with commerce, and (2) cataloguing needed to be both faster and able to prove real ownership. Each change is stated as Problem → Change → Why.
18.1 Separated the data model into Item / Listing / Post / Deal
Problem — The original spec treated a 'sale' as a type of post. That couples three different lifecycles: what a user owns, what they are selling, and what they say. A sold item's post becomes stale, the same item can't be relisted cleanly, and the marketplace can't reason about inventory.
Change — Four first-class objects: an Item (catalogue record of what you own/want), a Listing (a sale/trade offer attached to an Item), a Post (social content — showcase/discussion/review), and a Deal (a confirmed transaction). See §8.1.
Why — Decoupling lets each object have its own lifecycle and state. Items persist and power the collection, trust tiers, and wishlist matching; Listings open and close without destroying content; Posts stay evergreen; Deals give us a verifiable record of who actually traded with whom — the anchor for trust (§18.4).
18.2 Selling is an action on a collection Item, not a new post
Problem — The open question 'should a user create a post to sell, or mark a collection item as for sale?' exposed redundant data entry: a seller would re-describe and re-photograph an item they had already catalogued.
Change — Listing is initiated from the Item in the user's collection ('Sell' / 'Open to trade'); the marketplace is populated from Items, not from parsing posts (§8.6, screens §9.5).
Why — It removes duplicate effort, guarantees listings carry structured, consistent data, and ties every listing to a verifiable owned Item — which is what makes verification and anti-fraud possible at all.
18.3 Catalogue-first cataloguing for speed
Problem — Manual cataloguing (type everything, upload photos) is too slow and was the main adoption risk for the data spine the whole app depends on.
Change — Users add Items by searching/scanning a product catalogue (search, barcode/box scan) that pre-fills name, image, release, and reference value; manual entry is the fallback, not the default (§8.3, screen §9.4).
Why — The fast path turns cataloguing from data-entry into recognition-and-confirm, which is what makes a large, clean collection realistic — and a clean catalogue is what powers feed relevance, wishlist matching, and pricing.
18.4 Tiered ownership verification (Claimed / Shown / Verified) and transaction-linked trust
Problem — Catalogue data alone proves nothing about real possession, and the original vouch could be farmed by social endorsement (including by seeded/dummy accounts), inflating trust without any real trade.
Change — Items carry a verification tier: Claimed (self-asserted), Shown (in-app live photo), Verified (passed integrity/challenge checks). Listing requires Verified. Photo capture is in-app live camera only — no gallery upload — backed by challenge shots, perceptual-hash duplicate detection, and watermarking. The trade-vouch now requires a confirmed Deal; social endorsement is capped and weighted lower. Profiles surface concrete trust signals (completed Deals, rating, response time, account age, active listings). NFC and image-recognition verification are Phase 2 (§8.2, §8.3, §13).
Why — This separates the two questions verification actually answers — 'do you hold this item?' (possession) and 'is it genuine?' (authenticity) — and makes the easy path (Shown) cheap while reserving heavier checks for the moment they matter (listing). Anchoring trust to Deals means reputation reflects real trading and can't be manufactured by the seeding strategy, resolving a direct conflict between the dummy-account plan and the vouch system.
18.5 Wishlist → saved-search → match notification as the retention loop
Problem — The return loop relied mostly on broadcast (new releases, events). It lacked a personal, high-intent reason to return that the user themselves had primed.
Change — Wishlist Items become saved searches; when a matching Listing appears, the wishing user is notified (§8.3, §8.10). 
Why — A user-declared want matched to live inventory is the strongest, most personal re-engagement trigger and directly feeds marketplace liquidity — buyers arrive pre-qualified.
18.6 Follow as a first-class action; Interests vs Communities clarified
Problem — The original information architecture blurred 'interests' (topics that tune the feed) with 'communities' (groups you belong to), and offered no lightweight way to track a user, brand, or search.
Change — Follow is a first-class action across users, brands, communities, and saved-searches; Interests tune ranking while Communities are explicit memberships (§8.2, §8.4, §8.8).
Why — Following is lower-commitment than joining and produces a richer personalisation signal, while a clean Interests/Communities split keeps the feed legible and the navigation predictable.
18.7 Post types simplified to Showcase / Discussion / Review
Problem — With selling moved to the Item, a 'Sale' post type was redundant and confusing.
Change — Posts are Showcase, Discussion, or Review; commerce lives in Listings (§8.5).
Why — Each post type now maps to a distinct social intent, and there is exactly one place to sell, removing ambiguity for both posters and the feed-ranking logic.
18.8 Deal completion drives mutual rating and vouch
Problem — There was no defined moment at which a transaction was 'done', so ratings and trust had no trigger.
Change — Either party can mark a Deal complete from the listing or DM thread; completion unlocks mutual rating and the transaction-backed trade-vouch (§8.6 MK-08, §8.7).
Why — A concrete completion event is what makes reputation measurable and the §14 transaction funnel (listing → DM → completed Deal) trackable, replacing the weaker 'DMs initiated' and 'vouches issued' proxies.
